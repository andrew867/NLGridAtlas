# Newfoundland and Labrador public telephone system history

## Carrier structure

The public network did not develop as one company moving through a sequence of names. Two main systems expanded in parallel until 1988.

| System | Main service area | Succession |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avalon and major-centre system | St. John's, the Avalon and Burin peninsulas, Grand Falls, Corner Brook, Port aux Basques, and later Labrador outside Labrador City | Anglo-American Telegraph telephone exchange, Western Union telephone assets, Avalon Telephone, Newfoundland Telephone, NewTel |
| Rural and coastal system | Central Newfoundland and most coastal communities outside the first system's franchises | Newfoundland Department of Posts and Telegraphs, Canadian National Telecommunications, Terra Nova Telecommunications |
| Labrador City system | Labrador City | Labrador Telephone Company, associated with the Iron Ore Company of Canada until Newfoundland Telephone acquired the assets in 1979 |

Newfoundland Telephone acquired Terra Nova from Canadian National Railway on December 1, 1988. The companies amalgamated on January 1, 1989. CRTC Decision 90-14 confirms the acquisition date. CRTC Decision 90-15 records the purchase and amalgamation.

## Chronology

### 1878 to 1918, private instruments and the first public exchange

The first recorded Newfoundland telephone installation was a private St. John's line on March 20, 1878. It connected the homes of John Delaney and John Higgins. Delaney reportedly built the instruments from instructions in *Scientific American*. This is a later historical report, not a surviving installation record.

A second private line connected a Duckworth Street furniture business and its manager's Devon Row residence in 1884. Later sources disagree on the business description, so the atlas does not assign one.

The Anglo-American Telegraph Company opened the first reported public exchange in 1885. Its switchboard was above John Lindberg's jewellery store at 171 Water Street. The Great Fire destroyed the exchange in 1892. A replacement operated at 276 New Gower Street. Anglo-American sold the telephone system to Western Union in 1899. These early dates currently rest on the later *Encyclopedia of Newfoundland and Labrador* account. A contemporary 1885 opening notice and the 1899 sale instrument have not been located.

Private industrial exchanges also served mining, pulp and manufacturing properties. Later histories identify systems at Buchans, Millertown, Grand Falls, Bishop's Falls, Botwood, Bell Island and Corner Brook. Twillingate residents formed the Twillingate Telephone and Electric Company in 1913.

### 1919 to 1939, Avalon reconstruction and long distance

Avalon Telephone was organized in 1919 and acquired the existing St. John's system. Its franchise required a metallic-circuit, central-energy, multiple-switchboard system in St. John's with provision for at least 3,000 telephones. The agreement also required metallic trunk lines to Manuels, Brigus, Bay Roberts, Harbour Grace, Carbonear and Broad Cove. The 1938 statute reproduces the 1919 agreement.

Avalon completed the new St. John's system in September 1921. A 1924 account states that the plant allowed an ultimate capacity of 10,000 lines. The same account lists exchanges at Manuels, Holyrood, Brigus, Avondale, Bay Roberts, Harbour Grace and Carbonear.

The 1923 Board of Trade report gives a useful operating snapshot:

- 3,100 telephones in St. John's;
- 271 telephones outside St. John's;
- 41 city pay stations and 66 outside pay stations;
- 30 private branch exchange switchboards;
- two long-distance lines around Conception Bay;
- a submarine connection to Bell Island;
- a new all-copper line to Carbonear.

Avalon established radio telephone circuits among St. John's, Grand Falls, Corner Brook and the Burin Peninsula in 1937. A St. John's to Montreal radio channel opened in January 1939 and connected the island public network to Canada and overseas services. The surviving sources do not establish whether every internal HF circuit entered service on the same date.

### 1940 to 1959, automatic service, Confederation and microwave

A 1944 wartime account describes Avalon's Stromberg-Carlson switchboard, ducts, cables, toll circuits and radio links. It reported about 10,000 St. John's telephones, 1,600 Avalon telephones and more than 1,000 miles of toll line and cable. The network supported aircraft-detection reporting during the war.

St. John's converted to automatic dial service in 1948. Published user instructions defined the dial tone, busy tone, number-unobtainable tone, four-digit numbers with party letters and the ring-back procedure for party-line calls.

At Confederation in 1949, four organizations provided public telephone service:

- Anglo-Newfoundland Development Company in the Grand Falls area;
- United Towns Electric Company on the Burin Peninsula;
- Avalon Telephone on the Avalon Peninsula and at Corner Brook and Port aux Basques;
- Newfoundland Department of Posts and Telegraphs in central and rural Newfoundland.

Only three of 27 exchanges were dial offices. The rest used common-battery manual or magneto equipment. Some rural party-line systems had no long-distance connection. The off-island path was a single AM radio circuit between St. John's and Montreal that operated 12 hours per day when propagation allowed.

Canadian National Telecommunications inherited the Department of Posts and Telegraphs system. It began with a 50-line Gander dial exchange and rural party lines serving fewer than 500 subscribers. CNT installed step-by-step dial switching at Gander in 1951 and expanded automatic exchanges during the 1950s.

CNT completed a high-capacity microwave system between St. John's and Sydney in 1959. It carried telephone, television and data traffic and became the main intercity and off-island transport system. Avalon installed a separate microwave route from St. John's through Kenmount Hill to Bell Island and Bay Roberts in 1958.

### 1960 to 1978, dial conversion, DDD, electronic switching and two microwave networks

Bell Canada bought Avalon Telephone in 1962. Avalon opened a 20,000-line switching machine at Allandale Road in 1966. The cutover changed St. John's number prefixes and introduced Touch-Tone service.

Avalon became Newfoundland Telephone Company Limited in 1970. Direct Distance Dialing began that year. The 1977 government study says the first nine east-coast communities received DDD. A 1971 newspaper account says eight exchanges. The records do not identify the same count, so both are retained as a source conflict.

Newfoundland Telephone retired its last magneto offices, Western Bay and Old Perlican, in 1971. It installed its first SP-1 stored-program electronic toll switch at Corner Brook in 1974. That switch supported DDD expansion to the Corner Brook, Stephenville, Grand Falls and Port aux Basques toll centres.

CNT's rural program grew from 21 dial exchanges and 6,200 subscribers in 1961 to 63 exchanges and 14,000 subscribers in 1965. By 1967 CNT had 400 intra-island toll circuits. The company built microwave north to St. Anthony and south to St. Alban's, then expanded the St. John's, Gander and Corner Brook backbone to 1,200 channels in 1969 and 1970.

Newfoundland Telephone began its own cross-island microwave network in 1974. By 1977 it had also built the Goose Bay to Nain route and was completing the L'Anse au Loup to Goose Bay link. The St. John's to Cape Breton trans-island system entered service in 1978 with 1,200 voice circuits and television capacity.

CNT served 127 exchanges by March 31, 1977. Newfoundland Telephone served the denser regions and Labrador outside Labrador City. Labrador Telephone Company served Labrador City. The study's carrier map is the clearest located boundary record for the three territories.

### 1979 to 2001, digital switching, consolidation, fibre and congestion

Terra Nova Telecommunications replaced CNT's Newfoundland operation in 1979. It opened a DMS-10 digital exchange at Springdale on April 25, 1979. Terra Nova's 1984 company history called it Newfoundland's first digital exchange and the second DMS-10 in Canada. Newfoundland Telephone's 1981 annual report separately states that the company's first DMS-10 was installed at Nain in 1979. These are carrier-specific firsts, not competing accounts of one installation.

Newfoundland Telephone installed a high-capacity DMS-100 local switch at Corner Brook in 1980. It placed a second DMS-100 in service at St. John's on February 27, 1982. The St. John's switch included a remote line module at Torbay. Construction of another remote line module at Octagon Pond began in 1982. At year-end, 7.2% of subscriber lines were served by digital systems. The company expected the figure to reach 13% by the end of 1983.

Terra Nova used the same host-and-remote principle in central Newfoundland. Its Gander DMS-100 entered service in November 1985. The Glovertown remote switching centre was scheduled to cut over on October 15, 1986. It replaced an SA-1 crossbar installed in 1978, provided 1,000 lines with provision for 1,280, and connected to Gander by digital radio. The Gander program also included a DMS-200 toll switch. Eastport, Lewisporte and Gambo were identified as further Gander remotes, while Clarenville was planned as another DMS-100 host.

Newfoundland Telephone inaugurated the Corner Brook to St. John's section of its cross-island digital microwave network in June 1986. The 670 km route used 15 south-coast radio sites and fibre optic links at St. John's and Corner Brook. Construction had begun in July 1984. The company planned a final digital link to Sydney in 1987. This route was the digital transport layer that allowed host switches and remote equipment to exchange concentrated traffic without reproducing a full local switch at every exchange.

Newfoundland Telephone's records use two different remote equipment terms. The 1982 record calls Torbay an RLM, or remote line module. The 1986 record calls Glovertown an RSC, or remote switching centre. Nortel documentation shows why the names matter. The LCM is the subscriber-line concentration equipment. In the DMS-10 Generic 602.20 design, each LCM contains two line concentrating array shelves. Each shelf can hold five line drawers, and each drawer can hold 64 subscriber line cards. A 1979 DMS-100 RLM was functionally equivalent to a host line module, connected to the host over two to five DS-1 facilities, and offered optional intra-remote switching and emergency stand-alone operation. The later DMS-10 RLCM combined one LCM with host-interface, supervision and remote-maintenance equipment. The RSC-S added a remote cluster controller, local LCM capacity and optional digital trunk termination.

DMS-1U equipment followed another path into the same host. Nortel used a subscriber carrier module to integrate a DMS-1U remote terminal with a DMS host. In the DMS-10 Generic 602.20 design, the SCM-10U replaced the control terminal and the integrated remote terminal was called an RCU. The located Newfoundland carrier records do not yet assign a DMS-1U or RCU to a named exchange.

The conversion did not finish in 1986. NewTel's 1986 report said modernization would continue into the mid-1990s. CRTC Decision 92-15 records a later network modernization program that retired three SP-1 toll switches, transferred their traffic to existing DMS-100 and DMS-200 equipment, and made Mount Pearl the province-wide TOPS location. The located records show rapid replacement of named step-by-step and crossbar offices, but they do not yet identify the final Newfoundland electromechanical exchange or its cutover date.

Terra Nova completed a five-year improvement program in 1987 and reported 100% DDD availability and 99% single-party service for more than 50,000 subscribers in over 400 communities.

Newfoundland Telephone acquired Terra Nova on December 1, 1988. The companies amalgamated on January 1, 1989. Cellular service began in the St. John's area in 1990 after CRTC interconnection and tariff decisions.

Newfoundland Telephone began a $57 million fibre system between Sydney and St. John's in 1991. The submarine and Corner Brook sections were completed in 1991, the route reached Clarenville in 1992 and St. John's in 1993. By 1999 NewTel reported two Cabot Strait submarine fibre networks totalling 310 km. It classified 85% of its exchanges as high-cost because they served fewer than 1,280 network access services.

The network was not simply a smooth march toward better technology. The CRTC recorded more than a year of congestion on the former NewTel network in 2001, including serious difficulty reaching emergency services. Aliant advanced its Newfoundland Network Enhancement Plan to relieve the congestion.

## Technology sequence

| Period | Local switching and access | Intercity and off-island transport |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1885 to 1918 | Manual switchboards, open wire, private point-to-point lines | Telegraph-company routes and limited local trunks |
| 1919 to 1947 | Central-energy multiple switchboard, metallic subscriber and trunk circuits, PBXs and pay stations | Copper trunks, submarine Bell Island connection, HF radio from 1937 |
| 1948 to 1958 | Automatic dial, step-by-step and crossbar switching, extensive party lines | AM and HF radio, pole lines, narrow-band mainland radio |
| 1959 to 1973 | Dial conversion, common-control crossbar, Touch-Tone and DDD | CNT microwave backbone, Avalon regional microwave, expanded 1,200-channel CNT routes |
| 1974 to 1988 | SP-1 stored-program electronic switching, TOPS, DMS-10 digital switching | Newfoundland Telephone trans-island and Labrador microwave, digital microwave after 1986 |
| 1989 to 2001 | Consolidated provincial network, digital exchanges, cellular and competitive long distance | Cabot Strait submarine fibre, island fibre, remaining microwave and remote radio routes |

## DMS host and remote architecture

| Equipment class | Function in the located Newfoundland record | Dated installation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DMS-10 | Self-contained digital community exchange | Springdale, April 25, 1979; Nain, 1979 |
| DMS-100 | High-capacity local host switch | Corner Brook, 1980; St. John's, February 27, 1982; Gander, November 1985 |
| LCM | Subscriber-line concentration, two line concentrating array shelves, up to ten line drawers and up to 640 line cards in the Generic 602.20 design | Used in host and remote configurations; no named Newfoundland site assignment located |
| RLM | Earlier remote line module attached to a DMS host by DS-1 facilities | Torbay, in service by 1982; Octagon Pond, construction started in 1982 |
| RLCM | One LCM with host-interface, supervision and remote-maintenance equipment | No named Newfoundland site assignment located |
| RSC | Remote controller, LCM capacity and optional local trunk termination, connected to a host by digital transport | Glovertown, October 1986; Eastport followed in 1987 |
| DMS-1U / RCU | Pair-gain remote terminal integrated through a host subscriber carrier module | No named Newfoundland site assignment located |
| DMS-200 | Digital toll switch | Gander program, 1986 |
| DMS-100/200 | Combined local and toll switching platform | Mount Pearl, recorded in the 1992 modernization program |

The remote arrangement reduced the amount of subscriber traffic that had to be carried as individual analog circuits. Subscriber loops terminated on line cards at the local remote equipment. The remote concentrated the traffic onto digital facilities to a host DMS, where shared control, routing and service logic were available. The Glovertown record identifies digital radio as the host link. The cross-island network used microwave for the long route and short fibre links at its St. John's and Corner Brook terminals.

The Generic 602.20 manual distinguishes RLCM emergency operation from RSC-S emergency operation. An RLCM, RSLM or RSLE in emergency stand-alone mode can complete POTS calls only between subscribers on the same remote. Calls to the operator or outside the remote receive reorder tone, and the mode provides neither subscriber features nor recorded announcements. The RSC-S can terminate digital trunks and switch local RSC-S lines to those trunks, including during emergency stand-alone operation. No located record identifies the circuit-card complement or software release installed at Glovertown in 1986, so the later generic capabilities cannot be assigned to that site without further evidence.

## Source conflicts and open questions

1. The first public exchange is consistently reported as 1885 in later histories, but a contemporary opening notice has not been located.
2. The exact 1899 transfer instrument from Anglo-American or Western Union has not been located.
3. The 1977 government study dates the Corner Brook takeover and dial exchange to 1947. The later encyclopedia gives 1948. The atlas retains the 1947 date from the closer official study.
4. The first DDD group is nine east-coast communities in the 1977 study and eight exchanges in a 1971 newspaper report.
5. The CNT mainland radio route is described as a 12-channel system installed in 1950 in the encyclopedia and as a six-voice-channel system established by 1952 in later Terra Nova accounts. These may describe different equipment stages. The record does not yet prove the sequence.
6. The final step-by-step or crossbar exchange and its cutover date have not been located.
7. Complete exchange-equipment registers, route drawings and microwave hop sheets remain missing for several periods.
