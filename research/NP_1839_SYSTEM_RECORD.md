# 1839 System Record

## Halifax steam-mail plan

On February 8, the *Public Ledger* printed a Downing Street circular dated October 24, 1838. The circular proposed replacing the sailing mail vessels between Great Britain and Halifax with steam packets. It asked colonial legislatures to cooperate in improving onward communication. It did not report an operating steam route to Newfoundland.

Chapter 7, passed on October 12, records that an earlier £1,500 grant for a steamer between St. John's and Great Britain or Ireland remained unused. The act returned that money to the Treasury, then authorized £500 annually for three years for a steam vessel of at least 80 horsepower between St. John's and Halifax, or another convenient Nova Scotia port.

The operator had to provide a £3,000 bond, maintain the vessel, continue the service for three years and make two trips per month. Weather, coastal ice and other unavoidable accidents could excuse a missed voyage when accepted by the Governor in Council. These are conditional service terms. The act does not prove that a vessel entered service in 1839.

## Cape Spear flag telegraph

On May 9, the *Newfoundlander* described a proposal from Mr. Templeman of the Secretary's Office for a telegraph station at Cape Spear. Each vessel would carry a distinguishing signal and its mercantile house flag. Cape Spear would make the signal and Signal Hill would repeat it. The proposal was intended to identify vessels approaching from the south earlier and with less uncertainty.

A notice dated October 11 and printed by the *Public Ledger* on October 15 said that the committee appointed to erect the Cape Spear flagstaff had completed its arrangements. It said the telegraph would be put into operation forthwith. Private signals could be left with Mr. Shea, Secretary for the Lighthouses, and masters were to hoist their distinguishing flag when they came into sight.

The local system was an optical flag telegraph. The October notice supports completed arrangements and imminent operation, but it does not record a completed signal exchange or name an operating date.

## Published galvanic-telegraph engineering

On December 19, the *Newfoundlander* printed a technical account of the Great Western Railway galvanic telegraph in Britain. Brass keys acted by galvanic power on dial indications at the other end of the line. The indications included letters, numbers, punctuation and a correction mark.

The report described the completed Paddington to Drayton section as about thirteen and a half miles. Two wires passed through a hollow iron tube about one and a half inches in diameter. The tube ran about six inches above the ground and about two or three feet from the railway. A test question and reply were reported to have travelled to Drayton and back in less than two minutes. The article continued onto physical PDF p. 2, where it said that a child who could read could learn to transmit and receive after one or two hours of instruction.

The apparatus and test were outside Newfoundland. The article records detailed electrical-telegraph information available to St. John's readers, not a local electric line. No named 1839 Newfoundland electric-telegraph company, instrument, station or operating circuit was located.
