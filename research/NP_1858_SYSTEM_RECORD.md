# 1858 System Record

## St. John's company office

An April 19 notice from the St. John's office of the New York, Newfoundland and London Telegraph Company called a May 3 general meeting in New York. The notice identifies A. Shea as local manager.

## Cape Race news service

A March 24 article describes a vessel watching for mail steamers off Cape Race. News obtained from the steamers was sent over the electric telegraph from Newfoundland to New York. The article proposed a new floating message container; it does not establish that the proposed container entered service.

## Atlantic cable landing

Cyrus W. Field's journal records that the *Niagara* anchored in Trinity Bay at 1:45 a.m. on August 5. A shore party informed the people at the Telegraph House that the fleet was ready to land the cable. The cable was landed at 5:15 a.m. and the shore end was carried into the Telegraph House at 6:00 a.m. The journal reports a strong electric current through the cable from the other side of the Atlantic.

## August message tests

An August 16 message from Valentia reports that a directors' message was transmitted to Newfoundland and repeated back without error or omission. An August 18 message states that Valentia was receiving from Newfoundland accurately at 100 words per hour.

## October diagnostic work

An October 13 report states that the cable difficulty had not been removed. Mr. Lundy, an assistant at Valentia, came to Newfoundland to examine the wire at Trinity Bay and arrange coordinated signal tests from both ends. The report does not state that service was restored.

## November fault status

A November 5 report describes the cable as practically useless and says no fault had been found. Henley and Varley estimated that the fault was nearly 300 miles from Valentia. The estimate is not a confirmed fault location.

## Valentia test equipment

A December 7 account describes the test plant at Valentia. Henley's magneto-electric apparatus used two permanent magnets, each assembled from 30 horseshoe steel magnets. Each induction coil contained about six miles of wire. The article also describes a smaller receiving instrument and a galvanometer. It reports that the induced current from Henley's machine received a response through the faulty cable, but it does not identify the fault location.
